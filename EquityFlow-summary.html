PRODUCT DESIGN & DESIGN SYSTEMS

EquityFlow

Investing is a legal act. The interface has to carry that weight without scaring anyone off.

An equity crowdfunding platform where compliance had to feel like guidance instead of a wall.

Role Solo product designer (Carbonteq internship) Platform Desktop web Duration 5 weeks
01 · THE PROBLEM

A wall of forms before the interesting part

Before anyone can invest $100, the law asks them to prove who they are, disclose their income and net worth, connect a bank account, and stay inside an annual cap they've probably never heard of. Most platforms front-load all of it, and first-time investors drop out at the forms.

3
user groups with different goals: investors want speed, founders want visibility, operations wants a paper trail
4
legal requirements that shaped the product more than any visual choice
2
top-level tabs for the whole investor product: Campaigns and Portfolio
The reframe: the design problem was never the campaign grid. Each legal check is a moment where the platform has to say "no" or "not yet" to someone trying to give it money, so I treated each one as a copy and layout problem, not an error.
02 · THE PROCESS

Constraints were legal, so research came first

  1. 1Domain researchWeek 1 · Reg CF rules, caps, escrow
  2. 2Users & architectureWeek 2 · Three roles, two-tab IA
  3. 3Flows & wireframesWeek 3 · Grayscale, every state
  4. 4Harness & buildWeek 4 · Tokens, patterns, hi-fi
  5. 5States & QAWeek 5 · Empty, error, polish
  • Browse before verify.Anyone can open every campaign, its Form C and its Q&A. The identity and suitability checks only appear after someone picks an amount, when the ask has a reason attached.
  • Grayscale first.Every screen was solved without colour or real numbers, so reviews stayed on field order and what each screen was asking for. Colour later changed almost nothing structural.
03 · THE SOLUTION

Compliance as an interface problem

Each legal requirement shows up at the moment it applies, in plain language.

IDENTITY

A privacy note comes before the first field, and errors say exactly what's wrong.

SUITABILITY

Framed as a legal requirement, not a sales qualifier. No marketing copy.

ANNUAL CAP

A headline number on the portfolio. Over-cap amounts show exactly what would fit.

ESCROW

"In Escrow" is its own status, so held money never looks like a failed payment.

Campaign detail: terms and disclosures scroll on the left while the invest panel stays pinned on the right.

04 · DECISIONS I OWNED

Refuse early, and explain what would work

  • Two tabs, permanently.Watchlists, notifications and settings all wanted top-level slots. They went into the account menu so users never wonder where something lives.
  • Disabled over dismissed.When an amount breaks the cap, the button says "Fix amount to continue" instead of failing on submit.
  • Mono for every figure.Digits line up down a column, so amounts can be compared at a glance.
05 · THE DESIGN HARNESS

Stop building screens, write the system down

By the tenth screen the prototype had drifted: three button heights, two input borders, a nav with shifting padding. I paused screen work for two days and wrote a design harness: a short router file plus twenty pattern files, with no hardcoded values and a creation gate that says "stop and name the gap" instead of inventing something new.

It felt like losing time, and it was the reason the last fifteen screens took less effort than the first ten.
06 · REFLECTION

Write the system down before you scale the screens

The founder side and operations console exist as flows, not screens yet, and the product is desktop-only. If I ran it again, I'd test the suitability step with real first-time investors early: it's the screen where the platform sounds least like it's on your side.
