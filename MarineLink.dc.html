Marine Link

Most ocean content is informative. Marine Link is immersive.

An interactive, exploration-driven ocean experience that turns the five layers of the sea into a place to visit. Designed for pre-K and early elementary kids, Marine Link transforms a scroll into a deep-sea descent and every meter down into a reason to keep going.

The Idea

The ocean is one of the least explored and most exciting places on Earth. Yet, most ocean content treats learning like a test through dry facts and quizzes. 

Marine Link puts curiosity first instead. Most websites are too hard for non-readers and feel like schoolwork. This project works like the ocean itself: an immersive descent where kids discover one creature at a time.

Building the Concept

The process started on paper: hand sketches of a scroll-driven depth meter, a UI that darkens as you descend, creatures that only reveal themselves on interaction. Those sketches fed a Canva moodboard that locked our vision: a bioluminescent descent from daylight at the surface into near-total darkness, lit only by the creatures and the user's curiosity.

Early questions that shaped scope
  • How can we make the facts seem digestible and fun?
  • Should depth be a literal scroll axis or a menu?
  • Is there a gamified layer (progress, collection) or is exploration its own reward?

The Core Mechanic

Marine Link runs on a single, seamless loop: scroll deeper, discover a creature, and check it off your map. Making depth the navigation instead of tabs or menus kept the journey intuitive.

Features

Creature Detail Card

Tap to reveal, flip for the real thing

A tap opens a kawaii illustration with an interesting voice read fact. A second tap flips it to a real photo of the creature and a deeper fact. 

Find a Creature

Search or just tap a suggestion

Allows browsing for kids who do not know exactly what they are looking for. Results link straight to the creature map and searching for a creature takes you across the ocean to them making navigation easy.

Creature Map

Every zone, every creature, one screen

Helps navigation. All 35 creatures are grouped by zone with All / Unexplored / Explored tabs and a checkmark legend instead of red/green status. A "you are here" badge anchors to the player's nearest creature instead of floating in open water.

Quiz

A trophy, not a question mark

The quiz opens from a trophy, not a question mark since a "?" reads as help to a child and an unexpected test breaks that promise. Capped at 10 questions with a clear progress bar, it favors picture answers over text and a correct pick earns confetti instead of a wrong one earning a buzz.

Audio

 For the youngest end of the audience, audio is the primary way the app is used. Creature cards, search, and the quiz all carry a speaker icon with a visual pulse showing who's talking and search takes a tapped microphone in place of typing. Each zone also has its own ambient soundscape - brighter and airier at the surface, low and resonant by the trench - so the descent sounds like it looks, even before a child reads a word.

Visual identity

The logo went through dozens of iterations before landing on a soft, rounded, hand-lettered style. It is easy to read and playful enough to sit alongside creatures without competing with them.

Typography

Went through multiple rounds of testing, comparing bold versus regular, all-caps versus lowercase, and handwritten script versus a rounded sans-serif.

Our priorities with the text were that they must feel friendly, be easy to read, and complement the coral icon (and each other), making Fredoka and Lato the best choices.

Color system: one palette per zone

Instead of a single brand palette, each ocean zone has its own primary and accent colors to mirror a real descent. While most products avoid changing colors by section, here, that shift makes depth feel real.

Design principles

Simplicity
One core action per screen: scroll to descend, tap to reveal a creature. No secondary menus competing for a five-year-old's attention.
Contrast
Each zone's accent color pops against its darkening background, and status icons replace red/green so meaning never depends on hue alone.
White space
Creatures and UI elements get generous breathing room so nothing crowds the water around them.
Balance
The vertical scroll axis is the spine of the whole experience: progress meter on one edge, creature content centered, weight distributed evenly top to bottom.
Alignment
A reserved left-edge gutter keeps zone titles clear of creature sprites; facts and labels align consistently to the same left edge.
Consistency
One creature-card pattern, one progress-bar treatment, one reveal interaction, reused across all five zones so the child never has to relearn the interface.
Proximity
A creature's fact, image, and name sit together in a single card; the "you are here" marker is now anchored directly to the submarine icon it belongs to.
Repetition
The same descent ritual (scroll, discover, collect) repeats at every zone boundary, building a rhythm the child can predict.
Typography
Manrope for UI and facts at a large, bold minimum size; Cormorant Garamond reserved for headings only, keeping the reading layer distinct from the exploring layer.

Use of AI in the process

AI played a practical, hands-on role in the process. While concept work started on paper and in Canva, AI stepped in once the zones and creatures were locked. Gemini generated exploratory creature art and first draft facts. Claude Design (Sonnet 5) built the interactive prototype, handling the depth-scroll, zone transitions, and creature map. 

The first AI pass, before we fixed it

Claude's first build was rough: tilted, unstyled placeholders instead of finished art, broken alt-text standing in for missing images, a progress icon overlapping the counter, and a quiz button crowding the mute icon (as shown below). Getting from that initial rough draft to the final shipped screens took repeated rounds of re-prompting and manual fixes. The judgment call was ultimately mine while Claude simply helped execute the vision.

Building a design harness

Once the initial Claude prototype proved that ad-hoc prompting wasn't scalable: the fix was documentation, not more prompting.

I audited the shipped prototype's markup to extract every actual color, font, spacing value, and component state. From that, I built a dedicated spec featuring a component library (23 core components with required states), design tokens, a motion spec, and a voice guide in the design.md file.

Instead of guessing, Claude now had to validate everything against this spec before generating code. I enforced a strict creation gate: no new components or tokens without an explicit match. Any gaps were flagged for approval rather than improvised.

This resulted in new screens that matched the shipped app on the first pass, completely eliminating the manual correction rounds required earlier.

Decisions I owned

The target audience (pre-K through early elementary) came with specific constraints. Kids in this age group are still developing fine motor skills, easily get lost in complex navigation, and often view negative feedback as failure rather than a gentle nudge to try again. Every decision below was made against these hard constraints, rather than general kid-friendly intuition.

Per-zone color over a single fixed brand palette
It makes the descent legible with zero instructional text so a pre-reader can tell "I'm somewhere new" from color alone.
35 creatures, 7 per zone, not an open-ended list
An open list has no visible end. A fixed, countable one gives a five-year-old something to finish.
Kawaii illustration over a scientific rendering style
The audience needed creatures they wanted to look at, not study: a realistic anglerfish could scare a child off as easily as it fascinates them.
Depth-as-scroll over a zone menu
A menu would turn "you sink into this" into a settings screen and nested menus are where early-elementary kids get lost fastest.
A trophy for the quiz, never a question mark
A "?" reads as help to a child so a test behind it is a broken promise before the first question loads.
Correct answers glow, wrong answers don't buzz
Early-elementary kids disengage fast from negative framing. Success is confirmed loudly and a miss passes quietly.

Reflection & next steps

The current build proves the core concept: depth-as-scroll, per-zone coloring, and a countable creature loop make exploring feel genuinely distinct from reading. However, it has yet to prove the harder claim that exploring for fun and actually learning something are the same activity here. Content depth remains shallow beyond discover and admire and accessibility remains an active priority, not an afterthought.

Me exploring Claude Design!!

Working with AI on this project was deeply engaging but far from effortless. While it could generate a screen in seconds, it couldn't judge what felt right for a five-year-old exploring the ocean nor could it maintain a design system across dozens of prompts. The initial pass made this clear: colors drifted, components were needlessly reinvented, and every new screen required manual alignment.

The fix wasn't better prompting rather it was building a harness. By documenting the exact tokens, components, and states, subsequent prompts had a concrete source of truth to check against. That shift turned AI from an unpredictable tool into an aligned partner. Prompt engineering isn't a shortcut around design skill; it's a new way to apply it.

Next steps:
  • Give each creature a second layer of content beyond the reveal so discovery leads somewhere.
  • Run the accessibility fixes already identified (icon-based status, contrast, non-color-dependent cues) before adding new features.
  • Test with the actual target age group to see whether the fun vs. learning gap is real or assumed.
